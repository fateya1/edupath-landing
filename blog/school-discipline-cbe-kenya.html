Student Welfare and Discipline

Managing school discipline under CBE — a practical guide

July 1, 2026 | 7 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Discipline is one of the areas where CBE has created the most uncertainty among Kenyan school principals. The shift toward a learner-centred, competency-focused curriculum has been accompanied by a broader emphasis on restorative rather than punitive approaches to student behaviour. But the practical realities of managing 300 or 400 adolescents — with the full range of behaviours that entails — have not changed. Principals need an approach that is both educationally sound and operationally workable.

This guide offers a practical framework for discipline that aligns with CBE values, complies with Kenya's legal framework, and actually works in a real school.

The legal framework first

Kenya's Basic Education Act (2013) and the Children Act (2001) set clear boundaries on school discipline. Corporal punishment is prohibited. Expulsion requires BOM approval and must follow a formal process. Suspension requires parental notification and cannot extend beyond a defined period without escalation. These are not optional guidelines — they are legal requirements, and violations expose the school and the principal personally to liability.

A school that has a clear, documented discipline policy — approved by the BOM, communicated to parents and students at the start of the year — is a school that can defend its decisions. A school managing discipline informally, case by case, with no written record, is a school that cannot.

The CBE shift — from punishment to development

CBE's emphasis on holistic learner development means that discipline is increasingly framed as a developmental process rather than a punitive one. The question shifts from "what is the appropriate punishment for this behaviour?" to "what does this student need to learn to make a better choice next time, and how do we help them learn it?"

This does not mean there are no consequences. It means consequences are designed to teach, not just to deter. A student who damages school property is expected to repair or replace it — and to understand why that is the response, not just to accept it. A student who is repeatedly disruptive in class has a conversation with the class teacher and the deputy, identifies what is driving the behaviour, and agrees a plan — not just receives a detention.

Restorative practices in the Kenyan context

Restorative practices — originally developed in New Zealand and now used widely in Australia, the UK, and internationally — involve structured conversations between the student who caused harm, the student who was harmed, and relevant adults. The aim is repair of relationships and accountability, not punishment.

In the Kenyan school context, a simplified version of this works well: when a significant incident occurs, the principal or deputy convenes a brief structured meeting with all parties, facilitated by a neutral adult. Each party has the opportunity to speak. The outcome is a documented agreement about what will change. This approach resolves conflicts more durably than a unilateral punishment and leaves all parties feeling heard.

Documentation — the part most schools get wrong

Every significant discipline incident should be documented. Not for punitive purposes, but because:

  • Patterns become visible — a student who appears in the discipline record five times in one term is communicating something that needs a different response
  • Disputes can be resolved — when a parent challenges a disciplinary decision, a written record of the incident, the process followed, and the outcome is the school's defence
  • Regulatory compliance — if a student is suspended or expelled, the documentation trail is required by law

What to record: Date and time, student name, nature of incident, witnesses, action taken, parent notification (if applicable), and outcome. A digital discipline record that is searchable by student name and date range is significantly more useful than a paper book.

Involving parents constructively

The most durable discipline outcomes involve parents as partners, not as recipients of bad news. A parent who is called only when their child has done something seriously wrong — and who has had no positive contact with the school before that call — is a parent who is defensive and hostile. A parent who has received regular updates about their child's progress, attendance, and behaviour through a Parent Portal is a parent who comes into a difficult conversation already trusting the school.

This is why consistent, automated parent communication is not just a convenience feature — it is a discipline management tool.
