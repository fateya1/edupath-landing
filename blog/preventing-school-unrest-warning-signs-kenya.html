School Safety and Leadership

Understanding and preventing school unrest — early warning signs every principal should know

August 3, 2026 | 8 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Kenyan secondary schools have, in recent years, experienced periods of serious unrest — strikes, unauthorised protests, and in the most severe cases, arson. These events are traumatic for the students involved, damaging to school infrastructure and reputation, and deeply stressful for principals who often feel these incidents arrive without warning.

The research and lived experience of school leaders who have navigated these periods successfully point to a consistent finding: unrest rarely arrives without warning. There are almost always signals beforehand — signals that are visible if a school is actively looking for them, and invisible if it is not.

Unrest is usually the end of a process, not a sudden event

Reports into school unrest in Kenya over the past decade — including MOE task force findings — consistently identify a build-up period before major incidents. Grievances accumulate. Small conflicts go unresolved. Communication between students and administration breaks down. By the time an incident occurs, it is often the culmination of weeks or months of unaddressed tension, not a spontaneous event.

This is, in a sense, encouraging news for principals. It means the window for prevention is usually longer than it feels in the moment of crisis — provided the school has systems in place to notice the build-up.

Common root causes identified in Kenyan school unrest

While every incident has its own specific triggers, national reviews of school unrest in Kenya have repeatedly identified several recurring underlying factors:

  • Unresolved student grievances — complaints about food quality, facilities, treatment by staff, or academic pressure that are raised informally and never addressed
  • Breakdown in communication — students who feel unheard by the administration, with no structured channel to raise concerns
  • Perceived unfairness in discipline — inconsistent or opaque disciplinary decisions that students perceive as arbitrary
  • Academic pressure without adequate support — particularly acute around exam periods
  • External influences — drug and substance use, and in some documented cases, coordinated incitement from outside the school
  • Poor boarding conditions — overcrowding, inadequate food, or facilities that create daily frustration

Early warning signs to watch for

Behavioural signals

  • A noticeable increase in minor disciplinary incidents across multiple classes or dormitories, not isolated to one student
  • Groups of students becoming unusually quiet or evasive around staff, where previously they were engaged
  • Increased absenteeism or reluctance to attend specific activities
  • Rumours or complaints reaching staff informally through multiple, unconnected sources — a pattern worth taking seriously

Communication signals

  • Student leaders (prefects, class representatives) reporting that they are being asked to escalate concerns that have not been addressed through normal channels
  • Repeated informal complaints about the same issue — food, facilities, a specific staff member — without resolution
  • Parents reporting that their children have mentioned specific grievances at home that have not surfaced through official school channels

The critical insight from past incidents: In several documented cases of serious school unrest in Kenya, students and even some staff later reported that grievances had been raised weeks in advance through informal channels — but no structured process existed to escalate them to a decision-maker who could act. The absence of a functioning grievance system is one of the most consistently cited contributing factors.

What a proactive principal does differently

Principals whose schools have navigated periods of national tension without incident typically share several practices: they maintain regular, structured contact with student leadership — not just disciplinary contact but genuine listening sessions. They treat recurring informal complaints as data, not noise. They ensure that guidance and counselling staff have real access to students, not just a nominal office. And they communicate decisions and reasoning to students, even when the decision is not what students wanted — because a clearly explained "no" is received very differently from a decision that feels arbitrary or hidden.

Building the monitoring habit

A weekly review — even 20 minutes — where the deputy principal, senior teachers, and heads of dormitories or houses share what they are hearing informally from students creates a structured channel for early signals to surface. This does not need to be elaborate. It needs to be consistent, and it needs to result in visible follow-up action when something concerning is raised — because a monitoring process that identifies concerns but produces no response teaches students that raising concerns is pointless.
