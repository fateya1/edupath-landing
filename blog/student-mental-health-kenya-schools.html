Student mental health in Kenyan schools — a practical guide for principals

June 5, 2026 | 8 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Student mental health is no longer a topic that Kenyan school principals can treat as peripheral. The pressures facing senior school students today — academic competition, family economic stress, social media, and the weight of CBE performance expectations — are real and significant. Schools that ignore this reality do so at the cost of their students' wellbeing and ultimately their academic outcomes.

This guide is not a clinical manual. It is a practical framework for school leaders — what to look for, what a supportive school environment looks like, and when and how to refer students for professional support.

Why this matters now more than ever

Kenya's Ministry of Education has increasingly emphasised student wellbeing as a component of the CBE framework. More practically, the academic performance data from schools across Kenya consistently shows a correlation between student wellbeing and learning outcomes. A student who is struggling emotionally is a student who cannot fully engage with their education — regardless of their natural ability or their teachers' quality.

Senior school principals sit at a unique position of influence. They see students every day over three years. They have relationships with families. They manage teachers who are often the first to notice when something is wrong. Used well, that position is one of the most powerful mental health resources a student can have access to.

Early warning signs — what teachers and pastoral staff should watch for

The following changes in a student's behaviour or presentation warrant a pastoral check-in. None of them individually is diagnostic — but any significant change from a student's baseline is worth attention:

  • Sudden or gradual withdrawal from social interaction with peers
  • A notable decline in academic performance not explained by absences or assessment gaps
  • Increased absenteeism, particularly on specific days or before specific events
  • Changes in appearance, energy, or engagement in class
  • Increased irritability, emotional reactivity, or conflict with peers or teachers
  • Expressions of hopelessness, worthlessness, or disengagement from the future
  • A student who was previously active in school activities suddenly withdrawing from all of them

Digital data as an early warning tool: Attendance records and academic results, when reviewed regularly, can flag concerning patterns before they become visible in daily interactions. A student whose attendance has dropped from 95% to 70% over six weeks is telling you something through the data that they may not be saying in words.

What a mentally healthy school environment looks like

Mental health support in schools is not primarily about crisis response — it is about creating an environment where students feel safe, valued, and connected. The research is consistent: students in schools where they feel a sense of belonging have better mental health outcomes than those in schools where they do not, regardless of the academic pressure they face.

Relationships are the foundation

A school where every student has at least one adult — a class teacher, a subject teacher, a HOD — who knows them by name and notices when something is wrong is a school with a functioning pastoral system. This does not require a counsellor. It requires teachers who are given permission and time to have brief, human interactions with their students beyond the subject being taught.

Reduce stigma actively

In many Kenyan schools, a student who says they are struggling emotionally is at risk of being told to pray harder, work harder, or stop being weak. This response, however well-intentioned, closes the very channels through which students seek help. Principals who address this directly — in staff meetings, in school assemblies, in their own public language about student wellbeing — shift the culture over time.

Create structured pastoral touchpoints

Form teacher periods, class meetings, and one-on-one check-ins with students of concern are not luxuries — they are infrastructure. Schools that build these into the timetable rather than treating them as optional extras report catching student difficulties earlier and resolving them more effectively.

When to refer — and to whom

There are situations that go beyond what a school can or should handle internally. A student expressing suicidal thoughts, showing signs of severe depression or anxiety, or disclosing abuse or trauma needs professional support. In Kenya, referral pathways include:

  • Mathari National Teaching and Referral Hospital — Kenya's primary mental health facility, with outpatient services
  • Kenya Red Cross Psychosocial Support — community-based support in multiple counties
  • Befrienders Kenya — crisis support line: +254 722 178 177
  • Chiromo Lane Medical Centre — private mental health facility in Nairobi
  • County referral hospitals — most have psychiatric outpatient departments

When making a referral, involve the parent or guardian, document the referral, and follow up to confirm the student has accessed support. A referral that is made but not followed through is a referral that may never be acted on.

Supporting teachers' mental health too

Teachers who are emotionally depleted cannot support their students' emotional wellbeing. A principal who only attends to student mental health without attending to staff wellbeing is managing half the system. Brief acknowledgements, genuine appreciation, and a culture where it is safe for staff to say they are struggling are not soft management practices — they are the conditions that make everything else in this guide possible.

A well-run school supports the whole student.
