CBE and Education Policy

How Kenya's CBE compares to international education standards

July 1, 2026 | 8 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

When Kenya launched the Competency Based Education (CBE) framework, it was not starting from scratch. CBE is a global movement in education reform — adopted in various forms across Finland, Canada, Australia, Singapore, and numerous other countries over the past two decades. Understanding where Kenya's version aligns with and diverges from international practice helps school principals contextualise what they are implementing and why it matters.

This article is not a policy critique. It is a practical explainer for school leaders who want to understand CBE in a broader context — and who need to explain it confidently to parents, governors, and staff.

What competency-based education means globally

In its international form, competency-based education is built on three core principles. First, learning is defined by what a student can do — not by how long they have sat in a classroom. Second, assessment measures demonstrated competence rather than memorised content. Third, progression is based on mastery rather than age or time.

This stands in contrast to the traditional model — which Kenya's 8-4-4 curriculum exemplified — where all students progress through the same content at the same pace, are assessed primarily through terminal examinations, and are ranked by marks rather than competencies.

How Kenya's CBE aligns with international frameworks

Competency levels rather than percentage marks

Kenya's four-level grading system — Exceeds Expectation (EE), Meets Expectation (ME), Approaches Expectation (AE), Below Expectation (BE) — mirrors the competency descriptors used in Australia's ACARA framework, New Zealand's National Curriculum, and the International Baccalaureate's criterion-referenced assessment. All of these systems move away from percentage rankings toward descriptions of what a learner can do.

Pathway differentiation

Kenya's three senior school pathways — STEM, Social Sciences, and Arts and Sports Science — reflect a similar structure to the pathway or stream system used in Finland, where students choose between academic and vocational tracks from upper secondary level. The principle is the same: allow students to pursue depth in areas aligned with their abilities and interests rather than breadth across all subjects.

Continuous assessment

Kenya's CAT1, CAT2, and End-Term structure replaces sole reliance on a terminal examination with a continuous assessment model. This is consistent with international evidence that continuous assessment produces more accurate and equitable measures of student achievement than single high-stakes examinations — a conclusion supported by research from the OECD, UNESCO, and the World Bank.

Where Kenya's CBE differs from international models

Implementation speed

Most international CBE reforms have been implemented over 10-15 years with extensive teacher retraining, curriculum resource development, and assessment system redesign. Kenya's transition has been faster, which has created implementation gaps — particularly in teacher preparedness for CBE assessment methodology and in the availability of aligned teaching and learning materials.

Resource intensity

CBE models in high-income countries are typically supported by significant per-student investment in learning resources, classroom technology, and teacher professional development. Kenya's CBE operates under much tighter resource constraints, which means school principals carry a larger burden of implementation than their counterparts in, say, Singapore or Finland.

What this means for your school: The gap between international CBE best practice and Kenyan implementation reality is primarily a resource and support gap, not a design gap. The framework itself is sound. What schools that are implementing it well have in common is strong leadership, consistent data practices, and teachers who understand the assessment methodology — not better buildings or more money.

What international evidence says about CBE outcomes

The international evidence on well-implemented CBE is broadly positive. Studies from Canada, Australia, and New Zealand consistently find that competency-based systems produce better outcomes for lower-achieving students — because the system identifies and supports them rather than letting them fall behind and fail a terminal exam. High-achieving students perform at least as well as in traditional systems.

The key word is well-implemented. Poorly implemented CBE — with inadequate teacher training, inconsistent assessment, and weak data systems — produces worse outcomes than a well-run traditional system. This is why the quality of school-level implementation matters so much in Kenya's current transition phase.

What this means for how you explain CBE to parents

Parents who grew up under 8-4-4 often struggle to understand CBE grading. A student who receives ME in Mathematics has met the expected competency for their level — which is a positive outcome. But a parent accustomed to seeing a percentage score of 73% does not immediately understand what ME means relative to that.

The most effective explanation for parents: "CBE tells us what your child can do, not just how they compare to the class average. An ME grade means your child has demonstrated the skills expected at this level. An EE grade means they have gone beyond those expectations. This gives us much more useful information than a percentage."
