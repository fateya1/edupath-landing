School Safety

Emergency preparedness — what every school needs before a crisis, not during one

August 3, 2026 | 7 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Kenya has, in recent years, experienced several serious school fire incidents that resulted in tragic loss of life and highlighted significant gaps in basic emergency preparedness across many institutions. Regardless of the cause of an emergency — fire, structural issue, or other crisis — schools that have genuinely prepared in advance respond in minutes with organised evacuation. Schools that have not respond in confusion, and every minute of confusion in an emergency matters enormously.

This guide is deliberately practical and checklist-based, because emergency preparedness is not about elaborate planning documents — it is about a small number of things being genuinely in place and genuinely known by staff and students.

Fire safety fundamentals

Physical infrastructure

  • Fire extinguishers present, correctly charged, and inspected annually — not just present but functional
  • Dormitory doors that open outward and are never padlocked from outside while students are inside, under any circumstances
  • Clear, unobstructed evacuation routes from every dormitory and classroom block
  • Emergency lighting or at minimum, torches accessible to house masters for night-time evacuation
  • Smoke detectors in dormitories where the budget allows — increasingly recommended by MOE safety guidelines

Evacuation planning

  • A designated assembly point for every dormitory and classroom block, known to all students, not just staff
  • A fire drill conducted at least once per term, including boarding students during a simulated night scenario
  • A clear headcount procedure at the assembly point so missing students are identified within minutes, not discovered later
  • Designated staff responsible for specific dormitories during an evacuation, known in advance, not assigned in the moment

The single most critical safety rule: Dormitory doors and windows must never be locked or barred in a way that traps students inside during a fire. Multiple tragic incidents in Kenyan schools have specifically involved locked or barred exits. This is a non-negotiable safety standard that should be verified personally by the principal, not assumed to be in place.

Communication during an emergency

A pre-agreed communication protocol — who calls emergency services, who contacts the BOM chair, who contacts the Sub-County Director of Education, who communicates to parents — removes the need to make these decisions for the first time during an actual crisis, when clear thinking is hardest. This protocol should be written down, and the relevant staff should know their specific role in advance.

Emergency contact readiness

  • Fire service, police, and nearest hospital contact numbers posted visibly in the staffroom and principal's office
  • A pre-drafted parent notification message template ready to send via WhatsApp broadcast immediately if needed
  • Contact details for the BOM chair and Sub-County Director of Education readily accessible, not only in a filing cabinet
  • A designated staff member responsible for parent communication during an emergency, separate from those managing the physical response

Beyond fire — other emergency scenarios

Emergency preparedness should also cover medical emergencies (a clear protocol for when to call an ambulance versus manage on-site, and a designated first aid-trained staff member always on duty), severe weather events relevant to your region, and security incidents. The same principle applies to all of these: the time to decide the protocol is before the emergency, not during it.

Practising, not just planning

A fire drill that has never actually been practised is a plan, not a preparedness. Schools that conduct genuine, timed fire drills — including night-time drills for boarding schools, which are logistically harder but address the scenario where most tragic fire incidents have occurred — identify gaps in their plan before those gaps matter in a real emergency. A drill that reveals confusion or delay is valuable information, not a failure.

Reviewing after every drill

A short debrief after every fire drill — what took too long, which exit was congested, which students were confused about the assembly point — turns each drill into an improvement opportunity. Documenting these reviews also demonstrates due diligence that matters for regulatory and insurance purposes.
