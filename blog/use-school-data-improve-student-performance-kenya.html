School Leadership and Data

How to use school data to improve student performance next term

June 5, 2026 | 8 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Every school generates enormous amounts of data every term — attendance records, assessment results, fee payment histories, timetable compliance. Most of this data is collected, filed, and never looked at again. The schools that consistently improve year on year are the ones that treat this data as a resource rather than a record.

This guide covers the four most actionable data sources in a Kenyan school and how to turn each one into decisions that improve student outcomes in the next term.

1. Attendance data — the leading indicator

Attendance is the single most predictive data point for student performance. A student who attends consistently almost always performs better than one who does not, regardless of natural ability. This is not a surprise — but what surprises most principals when they first look at their attendance data systematically is how many students are consistently missing school in ways that were not visible from day-to-day management.

What to look for

  • Students whose term attendance rate is below 85% — these are your highest-risk students
  • Classes with unusually high absence rates — this may indicate a timetabling, teacher, or classroom environment issue
  • Patterns in when absences occur — Mondays and Fridays, or the week before fees are due, tell different stories
  • Students whose attendance has declined over the year — a downward trend is more concerning than a consistently low rate

What to do with it

Generate a list of every student below 90% attendance for the term. Share it with class teachers and pastoral staff before the holiday. Agree which students need a direct conversation at the start of next term. For the most concerning cases, contact parents before term ends — not to reprimand, but to understand and offer support.

2. Assessment results — the outcome indicator

Term results tell you what happened. The question is whether you use them to understand why and what to do differently.

What to look for

  • Subjects where a large proportion of students are scoring AE or BE — this may indicate a curriculum delivery issue, not a student ability issue
  • Individual students who are performing significantly below their peers across all subjects — these students need pastoral intervention, not just academic support
  • Classes where results have declined compared to the previous term — what changed?
  • Subjects where one teacher's class consistently outperforms another teacher's class in the same subject — this is a professional development conversation waiting to happen

The most important question to ask about results: Not "which students scored badly?" but "what would need to change for these students to score better?" The answer points to specific actions — additional support, teaching methodology changes, resource allocation, or pastoral intervention.

3. Fee payment data — the early warning system

Fee payment patterns are a reliable proxy for family financial stress, which in turn predicts attendance and engagement. A family that begins falling behind on fees in Term 1 and is significantly behind by Term 2 is a family that may pull a student out of school in Term 3.

Identifying these families early — not to pressure them, but to explore bursary options, payment plans, or referrals to government support programmes — is one of the highest-impact welfare interventions a principal can make. The data to identify them exists in your fee records. The question is whether anyone is looking at it with this lens.

4. Timetable and teacher deployment data

In schools with digital timetables, it is possible to see which classes are getting full curriculum coverage and which are not. A class that has had three different teachers for Mathematics in one term, or whose Science teacher has been absent 40% of the time, is a class that will have gaps in its learning regardless of how hard the students work. This is an equity issue — and it is often invisible unless someone is looking at the data.

Making data review a routine, not an event

The most effective schools build data review into their regular rhythm rather than treating it as a special exercise at the end of term. A brief weekly review of attendance rates. A mid-term look at assessment progress. A monthly check on fee payment trends. None of these takes more than 30 minutes — but together they mean the principal is always working with current information rather than discovering problems after they have become crises.
