Student Welfare

Bullying and peer conflict — the quiet driver of bigger problems

August 3, 2026 | 6 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Bullying rarely makes headlines the way major school unrest does, but it is one of the most consistently underestimated contributors to a range of problems that eventually surface more visibly — absenteeism, declining academic performance, mental health crises, and in some cases, collective student anger that has its roots in individual mistreatment that went unaddressed. A school that takes bullying seriously is, in a real sense, addressing a root cause of much larger problems before they develop.

Why bullying is often invisible to administration

Bullying typically happens in spaces with limited adult supervision — dormitories, dining halls, walking routes between buildings, and increasingly, through phones and social media even where phone use is officially restricted. Students who are targeted rarely report it directly, both out of fear of retaliation and because Kenyan school culture, like many others, does not always encourage students to report peer mistreatment as a legitimate concern.

Recognising the forms bullying takes

  • Physical bullying — the most visible form, but often the least common in practice
  • Verbal and social bullying — mockery, exclusion, rumour-spreading — significantly more common and harder to detect
  • Financial exploitation — older or more dominant students extorting money, food, or possessions from younger or more vulnerable students, a pattern documented in a number of Kenyan boarding schools
  • Cyberbullying — harassment through phones and social media, which continues outside school hours and supervision entirely
  • Initiation practices — informal, unsanctioned "traditions" imposed on new students by older ones, sometimes normalised within a school's culture over years without administration awareness

A specific risk pattern in boarding schools: Financial exploitation and initiation-style bullying of new or younger students by older students has been identified repeatedly as a contributing factor in student distress in Kenyan boarding institutions. This form of bullying is particularly likely to be invisible to staff because it operates within student social hierarchies that adults are not part of.

Building detection systems that actually work

Anonymous reporting channels

Students who fear retaliation will not report bullying through channels that expose their identity. An anonymous reporting mechanism — a suggestion box specifically for welfare concerns, or a private digital channel — removes this barrier, provided students trust that it is genuinely anonymous and genuinely acted upon.

House masters and dormitory supervision with genuine presence

Bullying that occurs in dormitories is far more likely to be identified by house masters who are genuinely present and attentive, rather than those who supervise from a distance or infrequently. Regular, informal presence in dormitory spaces — not just formal inspections — allows house masters to notice social dynamics that formal inspections miss entirely.

Peer reporting culture

Schools that actively teach students that reporting bullying — their own or witnessed toward others — is a responsible action rather than "snitching" build a culture where bystanders are more likely to alert an adult. This requires consistent messaging from the administration and, importantly, consistent follow-through that demonstrates reports are taken seriously and handled discreetly.

Responding effectively when bullying is identified

An effective response addresses both the immediate safety of the affected student and the underlying behaviour of the student engaging in bullying — which, particularly for repeat behaviour, often reflects the bully's own unaddressed issues. A purely punitive response without any effort to understand and address what is driving the behaviour is less likely to produce lasting change, though clear consequences remain necessary to protect other students.

New student integration as prevention

Much bullying, particularly initiation-style behaviour, targets new students during their most vulnerable period of adjustment. A structured, positive orientation programme for new students — pairing them with a trained peer mentor, briefing them clearly on who to approach with concerns, and actively monitoring their wellbeing during the first term — significantly reduces the vulnerability window that bullying tends to exploit.
