How to run an effective mid-year staff review in your school

June 5, 2026 | 7 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

The mid-year point is one of the most valuable and most underused windows in a school principal's calendar. Half the academic year has passed. There is still time to course-correct before the year closes. Teachers who are struggling can be supported. Those who are excelling can be recognised. Staffing gaps that will affect next year can be identified now, not in November.

Yet in most Kenyan schools, the mid-year staff review either does not happen or is reduced to a brief staffroom conversation. This guide outlines a practical approach that takes the process seriously without making it burdensome for anyone.

What a mid-year staff review is — and is not

A mid-year review is not a performance appraisal in the formal TSC sense. It is a structured, honest conversation between a teacher and their line manager — usually the deputy principal or head of department — about how the first half of the year has gone and what adjustments would make the second half more effective.

It should be developmental, not punitive. A teacher who feels the review is a platform for criticism will disengage from it immediately. A teacher who feels it is a genuine opportunity to be heard and supported will take it seriously.

What to review

1. Curriculum coverage

Has the teacher covered the scheme of work as planned? If not, why not, and what is the plan to catch up or adjust? This is the most objective element of the review — it is either done or it is not. Digital records showing which topics have been assessed and which have not make this conversation straightforward.

2. Student performance in their classes

What do the Term 1 and Term 2 results show about student learning in this teacher's subjects? Are results consistent with school-wide performance, or are there outliers in either direction that warrant discussion? This is not about blame — a class that is consistently underperforming may reflect issues with the timetable, class size, or resources rather than the teacher.

3. Attendance and punctuality

A teacher's attendance record is a factual data point that is easy to review objectively. A teacher who has been absent frequently, or who consistently marks registers late, needs a supportive conversation about what is happening — not an ambush, but an acknowledgement that it has been noticed and a genuine enquiry into whether support is needed.

4. Professional development needs

What does this teacher need to be more effective in the second half of the year? A subject refresher? Support with CBE assessment methodology? Better classroom management tools? The mid-year review is the right time to identify these needs and commit to addressing them before the year ends.

A useful question to ask every teacher: "What is one thing the school could do differently that would make your work more effective?" The answers are often illuminating — and act on them where possible.

How to structure the conversation

Each review conversation should take no more than 30 minutes. A simple structure works best:

  1. Start with what is going well — specific, genuine recognition of something the teacher has done effectively
  2. Review the data — curriculum coverage, attendance, student results
  3. Ask the teacher to identify their own development area — teachers who identify their own growth needs are far more likely to act on them
  4. Agree one specific action — one thing the teacher will do differently, and one thing the school will provide or change
  5. Document the conversation — a brief written record that both parties have sight of

Making mid-year reviews manageable at scale

In a school with 30 teachers, conducting 30 individual review conversations in the same week is not realistic. A practical approach is to conduct reviews by department over a two-week period — the HOD reviews their subject teachers, the deputy reviews the HODs, and the principal reviews the deputy and any staff members who require direct attention.

Digital staff records make this far more efficient. A principal or HOD who can pull up a teacher's attendance record, their class results, and their scheme of work coverage before the meeting is having a much more productive conversation than one who is relying on memory and general impressions.

The follow-through is everything

A mid-year review that produces agreed actions and then nothing happens is worse than no review at all — it signals to staff that the process is performative. Build a simple follow-up into your calendar: a brief check-in with each teacher four weeks after their review to see whether the agreed action has been taken. That follow-through is what makes the review meaningful.
