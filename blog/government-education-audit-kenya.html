Regulation and Compliance

What government education regulators look for when auditing a school

July 1, 2026 | 7 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

Every Kenyan school principal has, at some point, felt the particular anxiety of an unannounced regulator visit. What exactly are they going to ask for? What will they find? This guide demystifies the process by covering, plainly and practically, what MOE Quality Assurance and Standards Officers and TSC officials actually assess during a school audit.

The audit is not a mystery — it follows a structure

Regulator audits in Kenya are not arbitrary. They follow a defined assessment structure covering learner welfare, curriculum implementation, staffing, governance, and infrastructure. Understanding this structure means a principal can prepare systematically rather than anxiously guessing what might be asked.

Learner records and welfare

Regulators verify that every enrolled student has complete admission documentation, that enrolment numbers match NEMIS records, and that student welfare provisions — including discipline records, guidance and counselling access, and safety measures — are in place and documented. A school where enrolment records do not reconcile with what is physically observed in classrooms raises immediate concern.

Curriculum implementation

This is typically the most detailed part of the audit. Regulators check whether schemes of work exist and are being followed, whether assessment records show continuous evaluation consistent with CBE requirements, and whether teachers are delivering the curriculum for their assigned pathway competently. They may sit in on lessons, review lesson plans, and cross-check assessment records against actual coverage.

What raises red flags here

  • Schemes of work that exist on paper but show no evidence of being followed
  • Assessment records with large gaps or inconsistent grading
  • Teachers assigned to subjects outside their TSC registration or training
  • Classes where the syllabus coverage is significantly behind schedule with no documented reason

Staffing and teacher deployment

Regulators verify that teachers hold valid TSC registration, that they are deployed to subjects matching their qualifications, and that staffing levels are adequate relative to enrolment. They also check attendance records for teachers — a school with poor teacher attendance and no clear management response to it is flagged for governance concerns, not just an operational issue.

A common surprise for principals: Regulators increasingly cross-reference teacher attendance data against curriculum coverage. If a teacher has been absent frequently but the scheme of work shows full coverage, this inconsistency itself becomes a finding — it suggests either the attendance record or the coverage record is inaccurate.

Governance

BOM minutes, evidence of BOM approval for fee structures and major expenditure, and documentation of how school policies were developed and communicated are all reviewed. A school where the principal appears to be making unilateral decisions without BOM oversight is flagged regardless of whether those decisions were reasonable — the process matters as much as the outcome.

Infrastructure and safety

Physical infrastructure — classroom conditions, sanitation facilities, safety measures, laboratory and library provisions relative to the school's CBE pathway offerings — is assessed against MOE minimum standards. This is the most visible and least surprising part of the audit for most principals, since physical conditions are hard to hide or fabricate.

How to be consistently audit-ready

The principals who handle audits with the least stress are those whose records are accurate and current at all times — not because they anticipated an audit, but because they run their school with good data discipline as a matter of course. A digital system that keeps student records, attendance, assessment data, and teacher information accurate and instantly retrievable means an audit becomes a matter of showing existing evidence, not scrambling to produce it.
