Exam pressure and student mental health — preventing breakdowns before they happen

August 3, 2026 | 7 min read | By Edupath SMS Team

The pressure surrounding major assessments in Kenyan senior schools is intense and, in some documented cases, has contributed to serious mental health crises among students — including incidents that have made national headlines. This pressure comes from multiple directions simultaneously: parental expectation, school reputation concerns, peer comparison, and students' own anxiety about their future. Managing this pressure thoughtfully is both a student welfare responsibility and, in a very real sense, a school safety issue.

Understanding where the pressure comes from

Exam pressure in the Kenyan context is rarely just about the individual student's relationship to their own goals. It is compounded by school-level pressure to maintain or improve mean scores and rankings, by parental investment — financial and emotional — in a child's success, and by a cultural narrative that closely links exam performance to life outcomes. Recognising these compounding sources helps principals see why exam periods require deliberate management, not just academic preparation.

What schools can do — practical, low-cost interventions

Normalise stress and provide coping tools

A structured session, delivered by the guidance counsellor or an invited resource person, on practical stress management — breathing techniques, study-break balance, sleep hygiene during revision periods — gives students concrete tools rather than only being told to "manage stress" without guidance on how.

Monitor for signs of acute distress during high-pressure periods

Teachers and house masters should be briefed, ahead of major assessment periods, on what acute distress looks like: sudden withdrawal, expressions of hopelessness, significant sleep or appetite changes, or explicit statements of being unable to cope. Staff should know that these signs warrant an immediate, gentle check-in and, where appropriate, involvement of the guidance counsellor — not simply being noted and left for later.

Language matters: Framing a single assessment as "determining your entire future" — even when well-intentioned as motivation — measurably increases student anxiety without improving performance, according to educational psychology research. Framing assessments as one important step, among others, that a student can prepare well for tends to produce both better wellbeing and comparable or better academic outcomes.

Managing the school's own pressure on students

Schools competing on national ranking tables can inadvertently place enormous pressure on students through public displays of individual rankings, comparison between classes, or explicit messaging that links a student's worth to their score. Reviewing whether your school's own practices — mock exam result announcements, ranking displays, motivational rhetoric — are adding unhelpful pressure is a worthwhile, honest internal exercise.

Involving parents constructively during high-pressure periods

A brief communication to parents ahead of major assessment periods — encouraging them to support their child's wellbeing at home, avoid adding pressure through comparison with siblings or peers, and watch for signs of distress — extends the school's protective approach into the home environment, where students spend significant time during study leave periods.

Recognising when professional support is needed

Some students experiencing exam-related distress need support beyond what school-based guidance and counselling can provide. Signs that warrant referral to professional mental health support include persistent hopelessness, expressions of suicidal ideation, significant functional decline (unable to attend classes, eat, or sleep), or a student directly asking for help beyond what the school can offer. Schools should have a clear, pre-established referral pathway — county hospital psychiatric services, Befrienders Kenya (+254 722 178 177), or other local mental health resources — rather than improvising this under the pressure of an acute situation.

The Grade 12 leadership team's role

Class teachers and subject teachers of exam classes carry significant responsibility for the tone set during high-pressure periods. Teachers who communicate confidence in their students' preparation, alongside realistic expectations, contribute to a calmer classroom environment than teachers who use fear or intense pressure as a motivational tool — a distinction that matters considerably for student wellbeing during the most demanding parts of the academic year.
